Pricing in the IRA era: the calculus has changed

10 min read · December 2024

The Inflation Reduction Act is the most consequential change to U.S. pharmaceutical pricing policy in a generation. It is also the subject of more confused commentary than almost any policy change in recent memory. Cutting through what's actually different from what's hype is essential for any commercial team setting strategy now.

Here's a structured view of where the calculus has genuinely changed and where it has not.

What's actually different

Medicare can negotiate, for the first time, a defined set of drugs. Beginning January 1, 2026, the Maximum Fair Prices for the first 10 selected Part D drugs take effect. Discounts negotiated by CMS run from 38% to 79% off list price. The list includes Eliquis, Jardiance, Xarelto, Januvia, Farxiga, Entresto, Enbrel, Imbruvica, NovoLog/Fiasp, and Stelara. CMS estimated that, applied to 2023 utilization, the negotiated prices would have produced approximately $6 billion in net savings for the program—roughly 22% of net spending on those drugs.

The second cycle, with prices effective January 1, 2027, includes 15 more Part D drugs. The list includes oncology agents (enzalutamide, palbociclib, acalabrutinib, pomalidomide), high-spend chronic disease drugs, and notably Wegovy and Ozempic—the GLP-1 franchise that has reshaped metabolic-disease economics.

The selection criteria are statutory. To be eligible, a drug must be among the 50 highest-spend single-source products in Part B or Part D, must lack generic or biosimilar competition, and must have been on the market at least 7 years (small molecules) or 11 years (biologics).

The "pill penalty" is real and structural. Small molecules become eligible for negotiation 7 years post-approval; biologics, 11 years. This 4-year asymmetry has measurable consequences for R&D allocation. Industry groups argue—and a number of academic analyses support—that the differential creates a structural incentive to invest in biologics over small molecules, and to invest in late-stage small-molecule programs only if they can establish revenue maturity quickly. There has been bipartisan legislative effort to align the two timelines at 11 years; whether that change passes remains uncertain as of mid-2026.

Part D redesign has changed the rebate equation. Beginning in 2025, the Part D out-of-pocket cap fell to $2,000 for beneficiaries, and the manufacturer share of catastrophic-phase liability rose. The combined effect changes net pricing economics for high-cost specialty drugs even where they aren't directly subject to negotiation. Commercial models built before 2025 that assumed the old Part D structure are giving wrong answers.

Coverage requirements have expanded for selected drugs. Once a drug is selected for negotiation and a Maximum Fair Price is set, all Part D plans are required to cover it—including all dosages and forms. This eliminates a formulary-leverage tool plans previously held over manufacturers, and it has the secondary effect of expanding access for selected drugs whose coverage was previously uneven. Wegovy is the most-discussed example: previously covered by very few Part D plans for cardiovascular indications, it will be required coverage from 2027.

What's mostly hype

"The IRA will collapse pharmaceutical R&D." Industry rhetoric on this point has run hot. The reality is more measured. The IRA reduces the expected lifetime revenue of certain late-stage assets, particularly small molecules approaching their negotiation eligibility window. It does not change the economics of early-stage innovation in ways that warrant catastrophic predictions. Several large pharma companies have continued aggressive R&D spending and BD activity since the law's passage; the share-price impact has been mostly absorbed.

"Negotiated prices will fully capture the discount." The 38-79% discount range looks dramatic until you back out existing rebates. For drugs in highly competitive therapeutic classes, the net price after existing manufacturer rebates was already substantially below WAC. Independent analyses, including a Brookings paper using publicly available data, have suggested that the realized incremental savings versus pre-IRA net prices is meaningful but considerably smaller than the gross-discount headline numbers imply. The actual fiscal impact on Medicare is real; it is also less than a naive reading of the percentages would suggest.

"This is a uniquely punitive regime." Compared internationally, the IRA negotiation framework is mild. Most peer-country reference pricing systems impose price constraints that are significantly tighter than the Maximum Fair Prices CMS negotiated. The U.S. has moved from no negotiation to limited negotiation. It has not moved to European-style price controls.

Strategic implications for commercial planning

Lifecycle planning has to model the negotiation cliff. For any small-molecule asset launching now, the 7-year window to negotiation eligibility is a hard date. Lifecycle planning that assumed mature products would continue at WAC indefinitely is wrong. The new model: how do we maximize cumulative net revenue across years 0-7, knowing that years 8+ will be at a Maximum Fair Price that's likely to be 40-60% off?

Indication strategy interacts with eligibility timing. Drugs gain new indications throughout their lifecycle, but the negotiation-eligibility clock starts at first approval. An asset that pursues its broadest indication late in life will see that indication negotiated at MFP almost immediately. This argues for sequencing high-volume indications earlier in the lifecycle than has historically been optimal.

Biologic preference for new platforms is rational. The 11-year window for biologics versus 7 for small molecules creates a clear incentive in early development. For programs with viable paths in either modality, the biologic path now has a structural revenue advantage of approximately $2-4 billion in undiscounted lifecycle terms for high-volume indications.

Pricing strategy at launch faces a new constraint. The IRA negotiation considers, among other factors, comparative clinical benefit and unmet need. A drug that launched at a price disconnected from incremental clinical benefit is now exposed to a regulatory framework that explicitly weighs that disconnection. The implication: launch pricing should be defensible against a comparative-effectiveness argument, not just an analog-based benchmark.

Combination products and modifications matter more. The statutory eligibility criteria reference specific products. Modifications, line extensions, and combination products that meaningfully change the product profile may reset relevant clocks. The legal and regulatory specifics here are evolving and worth watching closely.

The takeaway

The IRA has changed the U.S. pricing environment in ways that matter strategically and that don't reverse themselves under any near-term political scenario. It has not delivered the existential change that the most heated rhetoric suggested. Treating the law as the new operating environment—rather than as a temporary aberration—is the starting point for sound commercial strategy in the second half of this decade.

The companies that built scenario plans around the IRA being repealed have spent two years not adjusting. The ones that built scenario plans assuming it stays are ahead.
