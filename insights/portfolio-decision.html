The portfolio decision no one wants to make

11 min read · December 2024

The hardest decision in pharma R&D isn't which asset to fund. It's which asset to kill.

Every organization knows this in principle. Every organization fails at it in practice. The portfolios you can examine post-hoc almost always reveal the same pattern: programs that should have been killed at the Phase 2 readout were continued for two more years, soaking resources from programs that should have been accelerated. The cost of this isn't measured in the program that died slowly. It's measured in the program that got starved.

Why killing is harder than backing

Three forces operate against honest portfolio prioritization, and they reinforce each other.

Sunk-cost loyalty. A program that has consumed $200 million across six years has a constituency. People built careers on it. Vendors are aligned. KOLs are engaged. The economic argument that none of that matters—that the only relevant question is the forward-looking expected value—is correct in finance theory and approximately never persuasive in practice. The decision-makers feel the $200 million. They cannot help feeling it.

Champion bias. Every program has a champion. That champion's reputation, compensation, and identity are entangled with the program's continued existence. The champion is also, usually, the most informed person in the room about the program's prospects. So the most informed voice is the most biased voice. The organizational structures that work in development—champions who fight for their programs—work against honest culling.

Optimism cascades. The forecast assumes positive Phase 3 data. The launch plan assumes the forecast. The hiring plan assumes the launch plan. The capital plan assumes the hiring plan. By the time the Phase 2 readout comes in marginal, an entire institutional infrastructure is leaning on the program's continued progression. Stopping the program means dismantling all of it. That dismantling is painful. The organization, sensibly, looks for reasons not to.

What honest reprioritization looks like

The frameworks for portfolio prioritization are not the constraint. Every pharma organization has access to risk-adjusted NPV, technical-and-regulatory-success-weighted NPV, real-options modeling, and portfolio-optimization software. The methodology is fine. The execution is where it falls apart.

The organizations that prioritize well share four practices.

Decision rules pre-committed at the previous gate. Before the Phase 2 readout, the team writes down what result would advance the program, what result would kill it, and what result would trigger redesign. The criteria are signed by the development committee and recorded. After the readout, the readout is compared to the pre-committed criteria. If it falls below the kill threshold, the program is killed.

This is not a perfect system, but it dramatically constrains the post-hoc rationalization that otherwise dominates. A team that wants to keep a program alive after a sub-threshold readout has to argue against criteria they themselves wrote six months earlier. That's a much higher bar than arguing against a hypothetical objection from senior leadership.

The portfolio committee is staffed against, not for, programs. In most pharma organizations, the portfolio committee is staffed by people who have functional ownership of programs in the portfolio. This is structurally biased toward continuation—everyone has a program they want continued, so everyone votes for everyone else's. The fix is to populate the portfolio committee partly with senior people who have no program to defend: corporate development, finance, strategy. They have the standing to ask the uncomfortable questions and no incentive to dodge them.

Forced ranking, not threshold scoring. Threshold scoring asks: does this program clear the bar to continue? The answer is usually yes—almost any program can be argued above almost any threshold by a sufficiently motivated champion. Forced ranking asks: of these eight programs, rank them 1-8 in expected value, knowing that the bottom three will be killed regardless of where the threshold falls. This forces the conversation to be about relative merit, which is harder to fudge.

Asset transitions, not asset deaths. The single biggest organizational obstacle to killing programs is the impact on the people who work on them. Pharma organizations that prioritize well treat program closure as a transition rather than a termination—the team rotates onto another program, the platform technology gets repurposed, the early-stage learnings get formally captured. This is not just humane; it's strategic. Organizations that punish the people associated with killed programs will discover that nobody wants to lead a marginal program—which means the marginal programs are the ones that get the strongest champions, which is exactly backward.

The conviction calibration

One last practice that distinguishes the organizations that prioritize well: they routinely calibrate their own conviction.

For every Phase 2 program, the development committee records a conviction probability—the probability the program will reach approval, in the committee's collective view, today. Six months later, twelve months later, eighteen months later, the recorded probability gets compared to actuals (or to the most recent state). Programs whose conviction has eroded but whose funding has held are flagged. Decision-makers whose conviction has been systematically too high are flagged.

This is uncomfortable. It is also the practice most associated with portfolio organizations that, over time, allocate capital well. The organizations that don't do this don't know how well-calibrated their own decision-making is. The organizations that do, learn.

The strategic point

Portfolio prioritization isn't about better analysis. It's about creating the organizational conditions under which the analysis can be acted on. Sunk costs, champion bias, and optimism cascades are not failures of insight. They are failures of structure. They get fixed by changing how decisions are made, by whom, and against what pre-committed criteria.

The decision to kill is harder than the decision to fund. The organizations that have figured out how to make it cleanly, quickly, and humanely have a durable competitive advantage that most of their peers haven't yet built.
