FORECASTING

Why most pharma forecasts are optimistic by design—and how to build ones that aren't

10 min read · March 2025

Pharma forecasting has more analytical horsepower behind it than at any point in history. Real-world data, machine-learning uptake models, claims-level analytics, AI-augmented physician panels, sophisticated payer-mix simulations. None of it has meaningfully improved accuracy.

The published evidence is bracing. A widely cited analysis of 1,700 forecasts across 260 drugs found that consensus projections were off by roughly 71% one year before launch and were still off by 45% six years post-launch. A separate review of 50 U.S. prescription drugs found that only 12% of forecasts came within 25% of actual sales, while 32% were overestimated by more than double. A peer-reviewed study of reimbursement-related forecasts in Austria reached the same conclusion: forecasts were systematically too high, and the inaccuracy did not improve over a decade despite better data and tools.

If methodology were the problem, accuracy should have improved. It hasn't. So the problem isn't the math.

The mechanics of optimism

Forecasts inside pharma serve a dual purpose. They are simultaneously a prediction and a justification. They predict what a brand will do, and they justify the resources allocated to make that prediction come true. Those two roles are in conflict, and the second one usually wins.

Walk through the lifecycle of a typical forecast. At the program-decision gate, the forecast has to clear a threshold to justify continued investment. A forecast that comes in 20% below expectations doesn't get refined—it gets killed, along with the asset it supports. So the forecaster, consciously or not, finds reasons to believe the higher number.

Move to the launch-readiness phase. Marketing has built a launch plan against a target. Sales has hired a force sized for that target. Manufacturing has scaled. The forecast has to support the resourcing decisions that have already been made. Adjusting it downward triggers organizational pain that nobody wants to own.

By the time external due-diligence consultants are brought in to "stress-test" the forecast, the number has been institutionally underwritten by every function. The consultants will identify risks. The risks will be acknowledged. The forecast will be modestly haircut. It will still be wrong.

The five biases hiding in plain sight

Anchoring on launch analogs that don't generalize. Every new oncology asset gets compared to Keytruda's trajectory. Most aren't Keytruda. Analog selection is where most optimism enters the model—and the bias is hard to spot because the analog is almost always real and recent.

The inside view. Forecasters tend to model bottom-up: addressable patients, share assumptions, persistence curves. This produces internally consistent numbers that systematically ignore base rates from comparable launches. The outside view—what does the distribution of outcomes look like for similar assets?—is rarely modeled at all.

KOL conviction inflation. KOLs are a critical input but a biased one. They tend to be enthusiastic about novel mechanisms, dismissive of execution challenges, and unfamiliar with payer dynamics. A forecast that weights KOL voice heavily without correction will run hot.

Pricing assumed, not stress-tested. Most launch forecasts assume the WAC price holds. With Medicare negotiation, biosimilar pressure, and hardening payer formularies, base-case pricing assumptions are increasingly fragile.

Competitive response modeled as static. Forecasts almost always assume incumbents and pipeline competitors behave as they do today. They don't. A real competitive forecast has to model the playbook competitors will run when your asset launches—not the one they're running now.

Building forecasts that survive contact with reality

The fix isn't a better model. It's a different governance structure around the model.

Decouple the forecast from the funding decision. The forecast cannot be the gate. It is one input. When the forecast is the gate, the forecast becomes whatever it needs to be to clear the gate. Mature organizations separate the "what do we believe" from the "what do we approve" by months and by people.

Build a base-rate library. For every major launch you forecast, quantify the realized outcomes of 8-12 comparable launches. Not selected analogs, but the full comparable set. The distribution of outcomes is almost always wider and lower than the forecast assumes. This is the outside view operationalized.

Force a distributional answer, not a point estimate. A point forecast is a lie of false precision. A distribution—P10, P50, P90—forces the forecaster to articulate downside scenarios in concrete terms. Boards that demand point forecasts get optimism. Boards that demand distributions get honesty.

Run a structured red team. Assign a senior commercial leader the explicit task of arguing the bear case. Not a perfunctory devil's advocate exercise—a full bear-case forecast with its own model, assumptions, and analogs. When the bull and bear cases differ by 3x, the conversation that follows is the actual forecast.

Track post-launch deltas and feed them back. Most pharma organizations do not have a formal mechanism for comparing original launch forecasts to actuals at year 2, year 4, year 6. The ones that do learn faster than the ones that don't. The discipline of forecast retrospective is what separates organizations that improve from organizations that don't.

The strategic point

A forecast that's accurate but uncomfortable is more valuable than a forecast that's optimistic and wrong. Most pharma organizations have not yet acted on this premise—because the optimistic forecast greases more wheels than the accurate one does.

The leaders who change this will not do it through better methodology. They will do it by changing how forecasts are commissioned, who reviews them, and what consequences attach to being right or wrong. That's not an analytics problem. It's a governance problem.

It is also one of the highest-leverage decisions a commercial organization can make. Forecast accuracy compounds. So does forecast inaccuracy.
