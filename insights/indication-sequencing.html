Indication sequencing: the decision that defines a franchise

12 min read · January 2025

Pembrolizumab is approved in more than 35 indications. It started, in 2014, in advanced melanoma after ipilimumab failure—a small, terminally ill patient population that almost no investor would have called the foundation of a $25-billion-a-year franchise.

That sequencing decision compounds. It still compounds today. The choice of melanoma first wasn't an accident, and it wasn't obvious.

Indication sequencing is the highest-stakes commercial decision in oncology, increasingly in autoimmune disease, and in any area where a mechanism plays across multiple tumor types or pathways. The decision is usually made too early, by people too close to the program, with too little structured analysis. And it's rarely revisited once made, even though the optimal sequence changes as competitor pipelines evolve.

Why first indication matters disproportionately

The first approval doesn't just generate revenue. It generates four things that compound:

Real-world evidence base. Once approved in the first indication, the asset accumulates safety data, dosing experience, and prescriber familiarity. This evidence becomes the platform for every subsequent indication's regulatory and commercial story. A first indication chosen for speed-to-data builds the evidence base faster.

Reimbursement precedent. The first indication establishes the price, the J-code, the payer infrastructure, and the clinical positioning that subsequent indications inherit. A first indication launched at a high price in a small population creates pricing latitude for broader follow-on indications. The reverse is much harder.

KOL relationships. The investigators who run the first pivotal trial become the first KOLs, the first published authors, the first society-presentation voices. The choice of first indication selects which KOL community you're embedded in for the next decade. That community then drives uptake in adjacent indications.

Competitive positioning. The first indication signals to competitors what the asset is. A program that launches first in a niche then expands looks different—commercially and to investors—than a program that launches first broad and expands deeper. Competitors react to what they see, not what was internally planned.

The four-variable framework

The right first indication optimizes across four variables, in this order:

1. Speed-to-data

Which indication can produce a clean, decisive readout fastest? Pembrolizumab's first indication wasn't chosen because melanoma was the biggest commercial opportunity. It was chosen because melanoma had the highest objective response rate signal in early data, the smallest enrollment requirement, and the fastest event accrual. A clean dataset in melanoma created the regulatory precedent and clinical narrative that made every subsequent indication faster.

Speed-to-data is undervalued in most sequencing decisions because it doesn't show up in NPV models. The model assumes all indications launch with similar evidence quality. They don't. A clean first dataset is leverage; a messy first dataset is anchor.

2. Evidence transferability

How much of the evidence generated in indication 1 transfers to indication 2? In immuno-oncology, response data in one tumor type generates testable hypotheses in adjacent types. In autoimmune, mechanism-of-action data in one disease informs the next. In rare disease, less transfers.

The right first indication is the one that maximally informs the second and third. A first indication that's clinically isolated—where the evidence doesn't extend to anywhere—is a strategic dead end even if its NPV looks attractive.

3. Payer signal

Different first indications produce different payer responses. A first launch in a high-unmet-need rare disease tends to clear payer hurdles with less friction than a first launch in a competitive class. The pricing established in the first launch becomes a reference point. Once a payer pays $X for the indication where the value story is strongest, the second indication's pricing has to be defended against that anchor.

Sequencing into the strongest payer-value indication first creates pricing latitude. Sequencing into the weakest one first creates pricing constraints that follow the asset for life.

4. Competitive density

Where is the competitive set thinnest now? Where will it be thinnest at launch? These are not the same question. An indication that's empty today may have three pipeline competitors converging by your launch. The answer requires a forward-looking competitive landscape, not a snapshot.

This is also where most sequencing decisions go wrong. Teams pick first indications based on current competitive intensity, not the projected intensity at launch—which is 3-5 years away.

What competitive intelligence has to deliver

Indication sequencing decisions require CI to do something it isn't usually asked to do: project the indication landscape five years forward, with explicit assumptions about competitor pipeline progression, regulatory timelines, and trial-readout outcomes.

This is harder than tracking current competition, and it's also where the highest commercial value is created. A CI function that can forecast where the competitive density will be three years from now—not where it is today—is a strategic asset. Most CI functions don't do this. They report on current state.

Re-sequencing as the program evolves

One more dynamic that gets missed: the optimal indication sequence is not static. Competitor readouts, biomarker discoveries, and reimbursement-environment shifts can invalidate a sequencing plan that was optimal eighteen months ago.

Mature programs re-evaluate sequencing at least annually, with a structured re-look that asks: if we were starting this program today, given everything we now know, what would the sequence be? When the answer differs from the current plan, it's worth understanding why—and whether the difference reflects new information or sunk-cost loyalty to the original plan.

The franchises that compound do this systematically. The ones that don't, plant the flag once and live with the consequences for a decade.
