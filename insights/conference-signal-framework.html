Reading the signal in conference data: a framework

7 min read · February 2025

A medical conference is the densest signal environment in commercial pharma. Three days of presentations, posters, satellite symposia, KOL panels, investor breakfasts, and corridor conversations. Hundreds of people gather information that is, on the margin, more current and more granular than anything they will see for the next twelve months.

Most of it gets wasted.

The default workflow at major conferences—ASCO, ASH, AHA, EULAR, JPM Healthcare—is well-established and largely unproductive. CI teams attend. They take notes. They distribute summaries. The summaries get read once and filed. The forecast doesn't move. The competitive playbook doesn't change. The launch plan continues unaltered.

This isn't a coverage problem. It's a framework problem. Conferences produce signal in three distinct categories, and most teams fail to distinguish between them.

The three signal types

Data signal. What was the readout? What was the magnitude? How does it compare to expectations? This is the easy category—anyone with a clinical background can summarize a Phase 3 readout. But the value isn't in the summary; it's in the recalibration. A drug that hit its primary endpoint with a hazard ratio 30% better than the consensus forecast is not the same drug it was last week. Most CI summaries note the readout. Few summaries update the forecast assumptions for that asset, the competitive set, the indication landscape, and the white space.

Voice signal. What did KOLs say in panels, in Q&A, in the corridor? More importantly: what did they say differently than they said six months ago? KOL sentiment shifts—the rate of change matters more than the absolute level. A panel that two years ago was framed around "promising data" and is now framed around "implementation challenges" is telling you something about where the consensus is heading. Voice signal is qualitative, but it's not unstructured: it can be coded, tracked across conferences, and trended.

Absence signal. What was missing? Which competitor didn't present? Which trial that should have read out, didn't? Which KOL who is normally on the lead panel was conspicuously absent or relegated? Which combination data was promised at the last conference and didn't materialize? Absence signal is the single most undervalued category in conference intelligence, because it requires having had a hypothesis going in. You can only notice what isn't there if you knew what to look for.

The framework

The teams that get the most out of major conferences operate a three-phase framework: hypothesis register before, structured capture during, forced quantification after.

Before: the hypothesis register

Two weeks before the conference, the CI team builds a hypothesis register. Not a list of sessions—a list of beliefs the conference will validate, refute, or modify. For each major asset of interest, the team articulates: what do we currently believe about this program? What evidence at this conference would change that belief? What evidence would confirm it?

The discipline is harsh: if the team can't write down what would change their mind, they're not actually paying attention to the program. The hypothesis register also flags the absence-signal targets—the things that should be there.

During: structured capture, not transcription

The default note-taking at conferences is transcription—write down what was said. This produces volume without insight. Structured capture is the alternative: every session generates one of four outputs.

  • Confirms hypothesis X (with specific quote/datapoint)
  • Refutes hypothesis X (with specific quote/datapoint)
  • New hypothesis Y emerged
  • Notable absence: Z was expected, didn't appear

Anything that doesn't fit those categories is interesting but not actionable. Capture it separately or don't capture it at all.

After: forced quantification

The single highest-value step is also the one most often skipped. Within five business days of conference close, the team is required to update specific numerical inputs in the forecast model: peak share assumptions for the affected assets, time-to-peak curves, indication launch sequence, payer-mix expectations.

If the conference produced no quantitative changes, the team has to write a one-pager defending why—what would have had to happen to move the numbers, and why none of it did. That document is the most valuable artifact the conference produces, because it forces the team to be explicit about what they actually believe versus what they were entertained by.

The tell

The simplest test of whether a CI organization extracts value from conferences: ask the head of CI what the team's forecast for a specific competitor's lead asset was the month before a major readout, and what it became the month after. If the answer is "the same number," the conference produced no signal—or, more likely, the team didn't have the discipline to update on it.

Conferences don't generate intelligence. The framework around them does.
