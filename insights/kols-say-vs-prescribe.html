PRIMARY RESEARCH

What KOLs say vs. what they prescribe: closing the gap

9 min read · February 2025

If you've ever sat through a KOL advisory board on a launching asset, you've seen the pattern. Twelve experts in a room, broad enthusiasm for the mechanism, articulate concerns about side-effect management, thoughtful suggestions on patient selection. The meeting concludes with the moderator asking each KOL: what share of your eligible patients will you start on this in the first six months?

The answers cluster optimistically. 40%, 50%, 60% of eligible patients. The forecasting team builds a launch curve assuming half that. The drug launches. Six months later, claims data shows starts at less than half of even the conservative number.

The KOLs were not lying. They were not even exaggerating, really. They were doing what humans do when asked a hypothetical question: answering with a model of themselves that doesn't match the model that actually drives their behavior.

The literature is unambiguous

This isn't a phenomenon discovered by pharma research. It's been studied across health economics for decades. A widely cited paper by Mark and Swait, published in Health Economics in 2004, used both stated-preference and revealed-preference modeling to evaluate physicians' prescribing decisions in alcoholism medications. The two methods produced parameter estimates that were statistically equivalent up to scale—but the scale itself differed, in predictable ways. Physicians overweighted attributes they could articulate (efficacy, mechanism) and underweighted attributes that drive real-world behavior (formulary friction, prior-auth burden, habit).

Twenty years of subsequent research has confirmed and extended this finding. The pattern is consistent across therapeutic areas, geographies, and physician specialties. Stated preference and revealed preference don't agree. They disagree systematically.

Six predictable gaps

The novelty premium. KOLs talk up novel mechanisms. They prescribe what they're comfortable with. The gap is widest at launch and narrows over 18-24 months as comfort builds.

The friction blind spot. Physicians genuinely don't know how much friction they tolerate before defaulting to the easier option. They underestimate the impact of prior auth, REMS, infusion-center scheduling, and formulary tier. Stated preference says these don't matter much. Revealed preference says they're often the dominant variables.

The patient-mix assumption. Asked about ideal patients for a new asset, KOLs describe patient phenotypes that are 5-10% of their actual practice. They then forecast share against the broad eligible population, not the narrow phenotype they actually have in mind.

The institutional inertia gap. Academic KOLs operate inside formulary processes, P&T committees, and institutional protocols that they don't always foreground when asked about prescribing. They imagine themselves as the decision-maker. They're a decision-influencer, often months in advance of the actual switch happening at the institution level.

The recency-of-failure asymmetry. A KOL who had a recent bad experience with a competitor product will overweight that experience. A KOL who hasn't yet had a bad experience with the asset under discussion will underweight that future experience. Stated preference is heavily flavored by what's salient in the moment.

The social-desirability filter. KOLs in advisory boards know which answers please the sponsor. Most don't deliberately tailor their responses, but most also don't volunteer the most critical view they hold. The structure of the room shapes the data the room produces.

How to design research that captures both

The starting principle: any single data type lies in a predictable direction. Stated preference runs hot. Revealed preference runs narrow (it shows you what's happening, not why, and not what would happen under different conditions). The answer is structured triangulation.

Pair every stated-preference exercise with a revealed-preference referent. If you're running a discrete-choice experiment with prescribers, you should also have claims-based behavioral data on the same physician segment for analogous decisions. The DCE tells you what they value in principle. The claims data tells you what they actually do. The gap between them is the calibration factor for your forecast.

Use behavioral elicitation, not opinion elicitation. "How likely are you to prescribe X to your eligible patients?" is an opinion question. "Walk me through your last three patients who would have been candidates for X. What did you actually start them on?" is a behavioral question. The second is harder to answer and harder to mis-answer.

Probe the friction explicitly. Ask, in detail, about the workflow that would have to occur for the prescription to happen. Who fills out the prior auth? How long does it take? What's the patient's likely response when told there will be a 7-10 day delay? Most KOLs, when forced to walk through the actual workflow, voluntarily revise their share estimates downward.

Validate with quantitative survey at scale, not just KOL panels. KOLs are leading-edge prescribers. The community physician who drives the bulk of volume thinks differently. Their stated preference is also biased—but biased in different directions. Triangulating across both populations corrects for some of the systematic error in either alone.

Build the post-launch feedback loop. The single most under-used learning asset in pharma is the comparison of pre-launch stated preference to post-launch revealed preference, by physician segment. Doing this systematically across launches creates an organizational base rate for the stated-revealed gap. That base rate is gold for the next launch forecast.

The practical takeaway

Stated preference is not unreliable. It's reliable in a specific way: it tells you what physicians believe about themselves. That's worth knowing. It just isn't the same thing as what they'll do.

The teams that consistently forecast launches well are not the ones with the best advisory board moderators or the most sophisticated DCE designs. They're the ones who treat stated and revealed preference as two different measurements of two different things—and design their research to capture both, then calibrate one against the other.
